Confidentiality in UK Emergency Medicine

A Quick Reference Guide for EM Doctors

The Core Principle is Not Absolute

Patient confidentiality is a cornerstone of medical practice, governed by common law and the Data Protection Act 2018 / UK GDPRThese laws provide statutory protection for 'special category data' like health information, requiring a lawful basis for processing.. However, it can be lawfully and ethically breached in specific situations.

When You MUST Disclose

Disclosure is a legal duty, with or without patient consent, in these cases:

  • Notifiable Diseases: Statutory duty to inform the UK Health Security Agency (e.g., COVID-19, TB, Meningitis).
  • Road Traffic Act 1988: Provide patient identity/address to police for traffic offences. Do not disclose clinical details without consent/court order.
  • Terrorism Act 2000: Inform police of information that may prevent an act of terrorism.
  • Court Orders: Must be complied with exactly. Failure can lead to contempt of court.

When You MAY Disclose

Disclosure without consent may be justified if it's in the public interestA complex ethical concept where the benefits to the public are judged to outweigh the harm of breaching confidentiality..

Harm to Others & Public

Justified to prevent:

  • Serious harm to the patient or others.

Safeguarding is Paramount

Child Protection

The safety of the child overrides confidentiality.

  • Promptly inform children's services, NSPCC or police.
  • Share all relevant information.
  • Seek consent only if it does NOT increase risk to the child.

Vulnerable Adults

Balance autonomy with duty of care.

  • Competent adults can refuse disclosure, even if at risk.
  • Disclosure may be justified to prevent serious harm to OTHERS.

Key Frameworks

  • GMC Guidance: The definitive professional standard. "Confidentiality: good practice" is essential reading.
  • Caldicott Principles Eight principles governing the use of patient information in the NHS, including 'justify the purpose' and 'use minimum necessary'. : Governs information use within the NHS. Principle 7: duty to share for care is as vital as the duty to protect.
  • Mental Health Act 1983: Specific obligations for information sharing for detained patients.

Documentation is Key

If you break confidentiality, you must be able to justify your decision. Always record:

  • What information was disclosed.
  • To whom it was disclosed.
  • The date and time of disclosure.
  • Your detailed rationale for the decision.
  • Any objections from the patient.
  • Advice sought from senior colleagues or MDOs.

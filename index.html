Confidentiality in the ED: can I share this?

A quick guide for emergency department clinicians in England

Four questions Common situations Must share May share How to share Sources

New since 30 September 2026: duty to share information about children

Section 16LA of the Children Act 2004 (added by the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act) now says NHS trusts must share information that is relevant to safeguarding or promoting a child's welfare with other safeguarding organisations, such as children's social care and the police, when it may help them. This includes early help, not only child protection. You do not need consent, and sharing under this duty does not breach confidentiality.

The only exception is when you judge that sharing would do the child more harm than not sharing. Share through your Trust safeguarding team and processes, as before.

Source: DfE statutory guidance Information sharing to safeguard children and young people (updated 10 September 2026).

Start here: four questions

Confidentiality is a legal and ethical duty, but it is not absolute. You may share personal information if any one of these applies (GMC Confidentiality, paragraph 9).

  1. 1
    Does the patient consent?Consent is implied for sharing with the team caring for them, unless they object. For anything else (police, solicitors, insurers, employers) ask for explicit consent.
  2. 2
    Does the patient lack capacity to decide?You may share what is of overall benefit to them (their best interests under the Mental Capacity Act 2005), for example telling family about their condition.
  3. 3
    Does the law require it?For example notifiable diseases, FGM in under-18s, terrorism, identifying a driver for the police, a court order, or the new duty to share about children.
  4. 4
    Is it justified in the public interest?Usually to protect someone else from a risk of death or serious harm, or to prevent or detect serious crime. Weigh it up and speak to a senior.

If none applies, do not share. If you do share, share the minimum necessary, tell the patient unless that is unsafe or would defeat the purpose, and write down what you shared, with whom and why.

Common situations in the ED

A police officer asks about a patient, or whether they are hereConsent first

There is no general duty to answer police questions. Without the patient's consent you should only share if the law requires it, a court orders it, or it is justified in the public interest (for example a serious crime against a person, or someone else at risk).

  • Ask the patient if they agree, unless that would put someone at risk or harm the investigation.
  • A written police request under the Data Protection Act 2018 (the "crime and taxation" exemption) allows you to share but does not require you to. You still need a reason from the four questions.
  • Speak to the consultant in charge. Share the minimum, and record it.
  • Police being at the bedside does not change confidentiality. Ask the patient before you talk about them in front of officers.

GMC Confidentiality paras 9, 17 to 19, 63 to 70 and 93. Data Protection Act 2018, Schedule 2, paragraph 2.

Police want to identify the driver after a road traffic collisionLaw: identity only

If the police require it, you must give information that may identify the driver alleged to have committed a road traffic offence. That means identifying details, such as name and address, not clinical information.

Clinical details still need consent, a court order or a public interest reason.

Road Traffic Act 1988, section 172(2)(b).

Police want a blood sample from a driver who cannot consentNot your job
  • The sample is taken by a police doctor or healthcare professional, never by anyone involved in the patient's care.
  • The doctor in immediate charge of the patient must be told first, and can object if taking it would harm the patient's proper care and treatment.
  • The sample is not tested until the patient later gives permission.

Road Traffic Act 1988, sections 7A and 9.

Gunshot wound (any cause), or knife wound from an assaultUsually tell police

Step 1. Usually tell the police when someone arrives with a gunshot wound, including an accidental one from a lawfully held gun, or with an injury from an attack with a knife, blade or other sharp instrument. At this stage do not usually give the patient's name or other identifying details. This lets the police assess the risk to the patient and others. You can ask another member of the team to make the call. If you are unsure how the injury happened, talk to an experienced colleague.

Step 2. Decide separately whether to share the patient's identity or other information. You can share it if the patient consents, if the law requires it, or if it is justified in the public interest: for example, others are still at risk, or it is likely to help prevent, detect or prosecute a serious crime. Do not usually share against the wishes of an adult with capacity when they are the only person at risk. If in doubt, the decision should be made or agreed by the consultant in charge or the Caldicott or data guardian.

  • Knife injuries that are accidental or self-inflicted should not usually be reported to the police. Reporting may also not be proportionate when only the patient is at risk.
  • Treatment always comes first. If the patient does not want to speak to the police, the team and the police must respect that.
  • If the patient is under 18, also follow safeguarding procedures.
  • Record what you shared and why, and the steps you took to get consent or tell the patient (or why you did not).
  • If you share without consent, tell the patient as soon as you can. Give no further information unless there is an immediate public interest reason; the police can apply for a court order.

GMC Confidentiality: reporting gunshot and knife wounds, paragraphs 5 to 20 (checked against the GMC page on 23 October 2026).

A girl under 18 has FGM (she tells you, or you see signs)Law: you report

You must report it to the police yourself by calling 101. Best practice is by the end of the next working day. Also follow safeguarding procedures.

  • The duty applies when the girl tells you, or you see physical signs that appear to show FGM.
  • If a parent or someone else tells you, or the patient is 18 or over, the mandatory duty does not apply. Follow safeguarding procedures instead.
  • Do not carry out a genital examination just to look for FGM. The duty applies to what you see during care you are already giving.

Female Genital Mutilation Act 2003, section 5B (England and Wales). Home Office Mandatory reporting of FGM: procedural information.

Suspected notifiable disease (for example meningitis, measles, TB)Law: notify
  • Notify on suspicion. Do not wait for lab confirmation. Consent is not needed.
  • Urgent diseases (for example acute meningitis, measles, diphtheria): phone your local UKHSA health protection team within 24 hours.
  • All cases: report on the UKHSA online service within 3 days.
  • Check the current list on GOV.UK. It includes COVID-19, food poisoning and scarlet fever.

Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2010. UKHSA Notifiable diseases and how to report them (updated 19 May 2026).

A patient is unfit to drive and says they will keep drivingPublic interest

The patient has the legal duty to tell DVLA. Tell them they must, and that you may have to tell DVLA yourself if they keep driving. Record your advice. If they disagree with you, offer a second opinion and advise them not to drive meanwhile; with their agreement you can involve relatives.

If they keep driving when they are not fit to, make every reasonable effort to persuade them to stop. If they still refuse and that puts others at risk of death or serious harm, you should contact the DVLA (or DVA) medical adviser promptly, without consent. Before you do, consider any reasons they give for objecting, and tell them you are going to. Afterwards, tell them in writing that you have done so, and record it. Involve a senior.

If the patient cannot understand the advice (for example because of dementia), tell the DVLA as soon as practicable.

GMC Confidentiality: patients' fitness to drive and reporting concerns to the DVLA or DVA, paragraphs 6 to 10 (checked against the GMC page on 23 October 2026). For the medical rules, see the EM Evidence DVLA tool.

A relative phones, or asks at the desk, how the patient isAsk the patient
  • Do not confirm that someone is in the department without their consent. Be careful not to confirm or deny things by accident.
  • If the patient has capacity, ask what they want shared and with whom, and record it.
  • If the patient lacks capacity, it is reasonable to assume they would want those closest to them told about their general condition, unless they have said otherwise.
  • You can always listen to relatives. Hearing their concerns is not a breach of confidentiality.

GMC Confidentiality paras 34 to 40.

You are worried about a childDuty to share

Share relevant information through your Trust safeguarding team and processes, with children's social care, and with the police if the child is in immediate danger. You do not need consent.

  • Since 30 September 2026 this is a legal duty, and it covers early help concerns as well as child protection.
  • Usually tell the parents and child what you are doing, unless that would put the child at risk or get in the way of an investigation.
  • Think about other children at home, including when the patient is an adult (for example domestic abuse, or a parent who is very unwell).

Children Act 2004, section 16LA. DfE Information sharing to safeguard children and young people (2026). Working together to safeguard children (2026).

An adult with capacity discloses domestic abuse but refuses a referralUsually respect it
  • Usually respect their decision, even if it leaves them at risk. Explain the risks, offer support (for example the hospital IDVA, or the National Domestic Abuse Helpline 0808 2000 247) and leave the door open.
  • Share without consent if others are at risk of serious harm, for example children in the household, or an abuser prepared to use weapons.
  • For high-risk cases (for example a MARAC referral), follow your Trust safeguarding policy and involve the safeguarding team.

GMC Confidentiality paras 57 to 59 and 65.

An adult who lacks capacity may be being abused or neglectedShare promptly

Give the information promptly to the right person or authority (your Trust adult safeguarding team and the local authority), unless it is not of overall benefit to the patient. If you decide not to share, discuss it with an experienced colleague and record why.

GMC Confidentiality paras 55 and 56.

A patient makes a credible threat to seriously harm someonePublic interest

Disclosure without consent can be justified to protect others from a risk of death or serious harm. Weigh up the risk (see "Public interest test" below), involve a senior and, where possible, your Caldicott Guardian. Tell the appropriate person or authority, usually the police. Share only what is needed, and record it.

GMC Confidentiality paras 63 to 70.

You learn something that might help prevent terrorismLaw: tell police

It is an offence not to tell the police, as soon as reasonably practicable, information you know or believe might help prevent an act of terrorism, or help catch or prosecute someone for one. Tell a senior as well.

Terrorism Act 2000, section 38B.

A court order, coroner, or a solicitor asks for recordsCourt: comply
  • Court order or coroner's requirement: you must comply. Share only what is required, and tell the court if you think something is irrelevant or sharing it could put someone at risk.
  • Solicitor, insurer or employer: you need the patient's explicit consent.
  • Get advice from your Trust legal team or your defence body if you are unsure.

GMC Confidentiality paras 90 to 93.

The patient has diedDuty continues

Your duty of confidentiality continues after death. If the patient asked for information to stay confidential, usually respect that. Otherwise weigh up whether sharing would help or distress the family, and what it would reveal about others. You must still help the coroner and meet the duty of candour. Requests for records go through your Trust's formal process.

GMC Confidentiality paras 134 to 138.

Taking a photo, or sharing a case in a group chatApproved routes only
  • Use only Trust-approved devices and apps for clinical images, with consent, and store them in the record.
  • Do not post identifiable details in messaging groups or social media. Remember that a patient can often be identified from the story alone.

GMC Making and using visual and audio recordings of patients. Your Trust information governance policy.

Looking up a colleague's, friend's or relative's recordDo not

Only open records you need for your role. Access is audited. Knowingly or recklessly getting personal data without the organisation's permission can be a criminal offence, as well as a disciplinary and GMC matter.

Data Protection Act 2018, section 170.

Must share: required by law

  • Children (since 30 Sept 2026): information relevant to a child's safety or welfare, to other safeguarding organisations (Children Act 2004, s16LA).
  • FGM in a girl under 18: report to police on 101 yourself (FGM Act 2003, s5B).
  • Notifiable diseases: to UKHSA, on suspicion (Health Protection (Notification) Regulations 2010).
  • Terrorism: information that may help prevent it (Terrorism Act 2000, s38B).
  • Identifying a driver when police require it, identity only (Road Traffic Act 1988, s172).
  • Court orders and coroners: only what is required.

Even when the law requires it, share only what is needed, and tell the patient unless that would undermine the purpose.

May share: public interest

When failing to share could expose others to a risk of death or serious harm, or to help prevent or detect serious crime. Weigh up (GMC para 68):

  • harm or distress to the patient, and to their future care
  • harm to public trust in doctors
  • harm to others if you do not share
  • the benefit of sharing
  • the type of information, and the patient's views
  • whether a less intrusive option would work

Ask for consent first unless that is unsafe or impractical. Get advice from a senior or your Caldicott Guardian, without naming the patient where you can.

If you share: do it safely

  • Share the minimum needed, and use anonymised information if that will do.
  • Check who you are sharing with (for example call the police back through switchboard or 101).
  • Tell the patient, unless that is unsafe or would undermine the purpose.
  • Record: what you shared, with whom, when, the legal basis and your reasons, any consent sought or objections, and who you asked for advice.

Everyday confidentiality in the ED

  • Curtains are not soundproof. Keep your voice down in bays, corridors and at handover.
  • Keep screens locked and whiteboards free of unnecessary detail.
  • Sharing with the team caring for the patient is expected and essential. The duty to share for care is as important as the duty to protect confidentiality (Caldicott principle 7).

Not sure? Ask before you share

Your decision must be one you can explain and justify. You do not have to make it alone.

  • ED consultant in charge
  • Trust safeguarding team
  • Caldicott Guardian
  • Information governance or data protection officer
  • Your medical defence organisation
